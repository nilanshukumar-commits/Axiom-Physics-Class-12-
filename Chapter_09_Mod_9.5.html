• Microscope ek nanhe object ko hamare paas (near point $D = 25\text{ cm}$) laakar use two stages mein mega-magnify karta hai (pehle Objective bada real image banata hai, fir Eyepiece use magnifying glass ki tarah aur bada karta hai).
• Telescope infinity par baithe vishalkay objects (Moon/Stars) se aane wali parallel rays ko collect karke focal plane par ek compact real image banata hai, jise eyepiece aankh ke paas laakar bada visual angle provide karta hai!

📐 02. The Core Law & Formulae

1. Compound Microscope

Two converging lenses: Objective ($f_o$ small, aperture small) + Eyepiece ($f_e$ moderate):

$$m = m_o \times m_e = \left(-\frac{v_o}{u_o}\right) m_e$$ $$\text{Near Point } (v = D): \quad m = -\frac{L}{f_o}\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right), \quad L \approx v_o + u_e$$ $$\text{Normal Adjustment } (v = \infty): \quad m = -\frac{L}{f_o}\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right), \quad L = v_o + f_e$$

Where $L$ is the optical tube length (distance between second focal point of objective and first focal point of eyepiece).

2. Astronomical Telescope (Refracting)

Objective ($f_o$ large, aperture large) + Eyepiece ($f_e$ small):

$$\text{Normal Adjustment } (v = \infty): \quad m = -\frac{f_o}{f_e}, \quad L = f_o + f_e$$ $$\text{Near Point } (v = D): \quad m = -\frac{f_o}{f_e}\left(1 + \frac{f_e}{D}\right), \quad L = f_o + u_e$$

Negative sign indicates an inverted final image relative to the distant celestial object. Normal adjustment provides relaxed, strain-free eye viewing.

📝 04. Comprehensive Stepwise Derivations Blueprint
Primary Board Derivation

Ray Diagram and Magnifying Power of a Compound Microscope

Fig 9.5A: Compound microscope ray diagram for final image formation at the Near Point ($D = 25\text{ cm}$).

1. The total magnifying power $m$ of a compound microscope is the product of the linear magnification of the objective ($m_o$) and the angular magnification of the eyepiece ($m_e$):

$$m = m_o \times m_e$$

2. For the objective lens, the linear magnification is $m_o = \frac{h'}{h} = \frac{v_o}{u_o}$. Since the object is placed just beyond $f_o$ ($u_o \approx -f_o$) and the real image is formed very close to the eyepiece ($v_o \approx L$, the optical tube length):

$$m_o \approx -\frac{L}{f_o}$$

3. The eyepiece functions identically to a simple magnifier. Its angular magnification is:

• Case (a) When the final image is formed at the Near Point ($v = -D$):

$$m_e = \left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right) \implies m = -\frac{L}{f_o}\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right)$$

• Case (b) When the final image is formed at Infinity (Normal Adjustment):

$$m_e = \frac{D}{f_e} \implies m = -\frac{L}{f_o}\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right)$$

Design Criterion: To achieve massive magnifying power, both $f_o$ and $f_e$ must be exceptionally small ($f_o, f_e \ll D$).

Primary Board Derivation

Ray Diagram and Magnifying Power of an Astronomical Telescope (Refracting Type)

Fig 9.5B: Astronomical telescope ray diagram in Normal Adjustment ($v = \infty$); tube length $L = f_o + f_e$.

1. The magnifying power $m$ of a telescope is defined as the ratio of the angle subtended by the final image at the eye ($\beta$) to the angle subtended by the distant object at the unaided eye ($\alpha$):

$$m = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$$

2. For small paraxial angles $\alpha \approx \tan\alpha$ and $\beta \approx \tan\beta$:

$$\tan\alpha = \frac{A'B'}{OB'} = \frac{A'B'}{f_o}, \qquad \tan\beta = \frac{A'B'}{EB'} = \frac{A'B'}{-u_e}$$

3. Therefore, the general magnifying power is:

$$m = \frac{\beta}{\alpha} = \frac{A'B'/(-u_e)}{A'B'/f_o} = -\frac{f_o}{u_e}$$

• Normal Adjustment (Relaxed Eye, Final Image at Infinity): Here, $u_e = f_e$:

$$m = -\frac{f_o}{f_e}, \qquad \text{Tube Length: } L = f_o + f_e$$

• Near Point Adjustment (Final Image at $D = 25\text{ cm}$): By lens formula for eyepiece ($\frac{1}{-D} - \frac{1}{-u_e} = \frac{1}{f_e} \implies \frac{1}{u_e} = \frac{1}{f_e}\left(1 + \frac{f_e}{D}\right)$):

$$m = -\frac{f_o}{f_e}\left(1 + \frac{f_e}{D}\right), \qquad \text{Tube Length: } L = f_o + u_e$$

Design Criterion: For large magnification, objective focal length must be large ($f_o \gg f_e$) and eyepiece focal length must be small.

Engineering Superiority

Reflecting Telescope (Cassegrain Type) & Advantages over Refractors

In modern observational astronomy, refracting telescopes suffer from severe fundamental limitations. The Cassegrain reflecting telescope replaces the heavy glass objective with a large parabolic primary concave mirror.

1. Zero Chromatic Aberration

Mirrors reflect all wavelengths according to the laws of reflection without dispersion ($r = i$). Hence, reflecting telescopes are completely free from chromatic aberration (color fringing).

2. Elimination of Spherical Aberration

By using a parabolic primary mirror instead of a spherical surface, all parallel incident rays converge to a sharp mathematical focus, eliminating spherical aberration.

3. Structural Mechanical Support

A heavy glass lens can only be supported along its outer rim and sags under its own enormous weight. A primary mirror can be supported across its entire back surface.

4. Massive Light Gathering Power

Fabricating large flawless glass lenses beyond 1 metre diameter is practically impossible. Mirrors can be cast in multi-metre diameters, delivering high resolving power.

⚠️ 05. Red Alert Exam Trap Zone

Trap 1: Microscope vs Telescope Lenses Swap

*Board Question:* Can an astronomical telescope be used as a compound microscope by reversing its lenses?
• NO!
• For a microscope: $f_o$ and $f_e$ are both very small (in mm or few cm) with $f_o < f_e$.
• For a telescope: $f_o$ is very large ($\sim 100\text{ cm}$) and $f_e$ is small ($\sim 2\text{ cm}$).
Swapping them will yield a magnifying power of practically zero or produce extreme aberrations!

Trap 2: Magnification vs Resolving Power of a Telescope

*Exam Trap:* Does increasing the magnification of a telescope increase its ability to distinguish close stars?
• NO! Magnification ($m = f_o/f_e$) only enlarges the angular size.
• The ability to resolve two close celestial objects is Resolving Power ($\text{RP} = \frac{D}{1.22\lambda}$), which depends strictly on the aperture diameter $D$ of the objective, not on magnification!

Trap 3: Normal Adjustment vs Near Point Adjustment

• Near Point ($v = D$): Yields MAXIMUM magnifying power, but the eye muscles are contracted and under accommodation strain.
• Normal Adjustment ($v = \infty$): Magnifying power is slightly lower, but the ciliary eye muscles are completely relaxed, allowing prolonged, fatigue-free viewing.

Trap 4: What Happens if Half of the Telescope Objective is Covered?

*Classic Question:* If the central half of a telescope objective is painted black:
1. The complete image is STILL formed; no part of the image is lost.
2. The brightness (intensity) of the image decreases by 50% because total light collection area is halved.

💡 06. Handcrafted Exemplars
Board Classic Level

Problem: A compound microscope consists of an objective lens of focal length $2.0\text{ cm}$ and an eyepiece of focal length $6.25\text{ cm}$ separated by a distance of $15\text{ cm}$. How far from the objective should an object be placed in order to obtain the final image at (a) the least distance of distinct vision ($25\text{ cm}$), and (b) at infinity? What is the magnifying power in each case?

Solution: Given: $f_o = 2.0\text{ cm}, f_e = 6.25\text{ cm}$, total tube separation $d = 15\text{ cm}$.

Case (a) Final image at Near Point ($v_e = -25\text{ cm}$):

$$\frac{1}{v_e} - \frac{1}{u_e} = \frac{1}{f_e} \implies \frac{1}{-25} - \frac{1}{u_e} = \frac{1}{6.25} \implies \frac{1}{u_e} = -\frac{1}{25} - \frac{1}{6.25} = -\frac{1 + 4}{25} = -\frac{5}{25} = -\frac{1}{5}$$ $$u_e = -5.0\text{ cm}$$

Since $v_o + |u_e| = 15\text{ cm} \implies v_o = 15 - 5.0 = 10.0\text{ cm}$. Now for objective:

$$\frac{1}{v_o} - \frac{1}{u_o} = \frac{1}{f_o} \implies \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{u_o} = \frac{1}{2.0} \implies \frac{1}{u_o} = \frac{1}{10} - \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1 - 5}{10} = -\frac{4}{10} \implies u_o = -2.5\text{ cm}$$

Magnifying power:

$$m = \left(-\frac{v_o}{|u_o|}\right)\left(1 + \frac{D}{f_e}\right) = \left(-\frac{10}{2.5}\right)\left(1 + \frac{25}{6.25}\right) = (-4) \times (1 + 4) = -20$$

Case (b) Final image at Infinity (Normal Adjustment):

Here $u_e = f_e = 6.25\text{ cm} \implies v_o = 15 - 6.25 = 8.75\text{ cm}$.

$$\frac{1}{u_o} = \frac{1}{v_o} - \frac{1}{f_o} = \frac{1}{8.75} - \frac{1}{2.0} = \frac{2 - 8.75}{17.5} = -\frac{6.75}{17.5} \implies u_o = -\frac{17.5}{6.75} \approx -2.59\text{ cm}$$
$$m = \left(-\frac{v_o}{|u_o|}\right)\left(\frac{D}{f_e}\right) = \left(-\frac{8.75}{2.59}\right)\left(\frac{25}{6.25}\right) \approx (-3.38) \times (4) \approx -13.5$$
JEE/NEET Competitive Trap

Problem: A small telescope has an objective lens of focal length $144\text{ cm}$ and an eyepiece of focal length $6.0\text{ cm}$. (a) What is the magnifying power of the telescope in normal adjustment? (b) What is the separation between the objective and the eyepiece? (c) If this telescope is used to view a $100\text{ m}$ tall tower $3\text{ km}$ away, what is the height of the intermediate image of the tower formed by the objective lens?

Solution: $f_o = 144\text{ cm} = 1.44\text{ m}, f_e = 6.0\text{ cm} = 0.06\text{ m}$.

1. Magnifying power in normal adjustment:

$$m = -\frac{f_o}{f_e} = -\frac{144\text{ cm}}{6.0\text{ cm}} = -24$$

2. Separation (Tube length $L$):

$$L = f_o + f_e = 144 + 6 = 150\text{ cm} = 1.50\text{ m}$$

3. Height of tower $h = 100\text{ m}$, distance $u = 3\text{ km} = 3000\text{ m}$.

Angle subtended by tower at objective:

$$\alpha = \frac{h}{u} = \frac{100\text{ m}}{3000\text{ m}} = \frac{1}{30}\text{ radian}$$

Since the intermediate image is formed at the focal plane ($v \approx f_o$):

$$h' = \alpha \times f_o = \left(\frac{1}{30}\text{ rad}\right) \times 144\text{ cm} = 4.8\text{ cm}$$
