Geometry of constant voltage envelopes, gradient relation $E = -dV/dr$, zero work theorems, and system binding energy.

⚡ 01. The Spark (Analogy)

Topographic map par bane contour lines ko dekho: ek hi contour line par chalte waqt aapki altitude bilkul constant rehti hai, isliye gravity ke khilaaf zero effort lagta hai. Theek waise hi, Equipotential Surface ek aisa 3D ya 2D envelope hai jiske har point par electric potential $V$ ek barabar hota hai. Kisi charge ko is surface par aage-peeche ghumane par net work done hamesha ZERO hota hai! Iska sabse bada signature rule: Electric field lines hamesha is surface ke strictly perpendicular (90°) hoti hain.

📐 02. The Core Law & Formulae

Equipotential Surface Properties

Zero work done moving test charge $q_0$ between points $A$ and $B$[cite: 13]:

$$W_{A \to B} = q_0(V_B - V_A) = 0 \quad (\because V_A = V_B)$$ $$\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} = 0 \implies \vec{E} \perp \text{Equipotential Surface}$$

Equipotential surfaces never intersect each other (otherwise two potential values would exist at one point)[cite: 13].

Potential Gradient & System PE

Differential field relation and interaction energy of two point charges[cite: 13]:

$$E = -\frac{dV}{dr}, \quad U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$

Negative sign proves $\vec{E}$ points in the direction where potential decreases steepest[cite: 13].

📝 04. Stepwise Derivations Blueprint[cite: 13]
Derivation A

Relation between Electric Field and Potential Gradient ($E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$)[cite: 13]

1. Consider two closely spaced equipotential surfaces $A$ and $B$ separated by a small perpendicular distance $dr$, with potentials $V$ and $V - dV$[cite: 13].

2. A unit positive test charge ($q_0 = +1\text{ C}$) is moved from surface $B$ to surface $A$ against the electric field $\vec{E}$[cite: 13].

3. External force applied to balance electrostatic force: $\vec{F}_{\text{ext}} = -q_0 \vec{E} = -\vec{E}$.

4. Work done by the external agent across distance $dr$[cite: 13]:

$$dW_{\text{ext}} = \vec{F}_{\text{ext}} \cdot d\vec{r} = -E\,dr$$

5. By definition, this work equals the potential difference $V_A - V_B$[cite: 13]:

$$V_A - V_B = V - (V - dV) = dV$$

6. Equating both expressions:

$$dV = -E\,dr \implies E = -\frac{dV}{dr}$$

Two Crucial Inferences: (i) Electric field points in the direction where potential decreases fastest[cite: 13]. (ii) Magnitude of $\vec{E}$ equals potential change per unit displacement normal to the equipotential surface ($dr = \frac{-dV}{E}$, meaning surfaces crowd closer in stronger field regions)[cite: 13].

Derivation B

Potential Energy of a System of Two Point Charges in Vacuum[cite: 13]

1. Consider two charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ initially at infinite separation. Bring $q_1$ first to position $\vec{r}_1$[cite: 13]. Since no external field exists initially, work done $W_1 = 0$[cite: 13].

2. Charge $q_1$ produces electric potential at any point at distance $r_{12}$[cite: 13]:

$$V_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r_{12}}$$

3. Now bring $q_2$ from infinity to position $\vec{r}_2$ at separation $r_{12}$ from $q_1$[cite: 13]. Work done against the potential of $q_1$[cite: 13]:

$$W_2 = q_2 V_1 = q_2 \left( \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1}{r_{12}} \right) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$

4. Total external work is stored as the electrostatic potential energy $U$ of the two-charge configuration[cite: 13]:

$$U = W_1 + W_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}$$

Sign Rule: If charges are like ($q_1q_2 > 0$), $U > 0$ (repulsive system, work done to bring them together). If charges are opposite ($q_1q_2 < 0$), $U < 0$ (attractive bound system)[cite: 13].

⚠️ 05. Red Alert Exam Trap Zone

Trap 1: Surface Shape Misconceptions

Students often assume equipotential surfaces are always spheres. Shape strictly follows field symmetry:
• Point charge $\implies$ Concentric spheres[cite: 13]
• Infinite straight line charge $\implies$ Coaxial cylinders[cite: 13]
• Uniform electric field $\implies$ Equispaced parallel planes[cite: 13]

Trap 2: Spacing of Concentric Surfaces for a Point Charge

For equal potential drops ($\Delta V = 10\text{ V}$), equipotential spheres of a point charge are NOT equidistant! Since $E \propto 1/r^2$, $dr = \frac{dV}{E} \propto r^2$. The gap between successive equipotential spheres increases rapidly as you move away from the charge.

Trap 3: Pair Counting Trap in Potential Energy of System

For an $N$-charge system, students often miss combinations. The total number of interaction terms is strictly $\frac{N(N-1)}{2}$. For 3 charges: 3 pairs ($U_{12} + U_{23} + U_{13}$). For 4 charges in a square: 6 pairs (4 sides $+ 2\text{ diagonals}$). Never double-count or omit diagonal pairs!

Trap 4: Conductor Surface & Volume Equipotentiality

Because $\vec{E} = 0$ everywhere inside an electrostatic conductor, $dV = 0$ throughout its volume[cite: 13]. Therefore, the entire bulk of a conductor is an equipotential volume, and its surface is an equipotential surface[cite: 13].

💡 06. Handcrafted Exemplars
Board Classic Level

Problem: Three point charges $+q$, $+2q$, and $+4q$ are placed at the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side $a$. Find the total electrostatic potential energy of the configuration, and determine the work required to double the side length of the triangle.

Solution:

1. Total potential energy is the sum over all unique pairs ($N=3 \implies \frac{3 \times 2}{2} = 3$ pairs):

$$U_i = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[ \frac{(q)(2q)}{a} + \frac{(2q)(4q)}{a} + \frac{(q)(4q)}{a} \right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}[2q^2 + 8q^2 + 4q^2] = \frac{14q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}$$

2. When side length is doubled ($a' = 2a$):

$$U_f = \frac{14q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 (2a)} = \frac{7q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}$$

3. Work required by external agent $W_{\text{ext}} = \Delta U = U_f - U_i$:

$$W_{\text{ext}} = \frac{7q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a} - \frac{14q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a} = -\frac{7q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a}$$

Physical Insight: Negative work indicates like charges naturally repel and push each other apart; external agent must restrain them.

JEE/NEET Competitive Trap

Problem: A uniform electric field exists in space making an angle of $30^\circ$ with the $+x$-axis in the $xy$-plane, with magnitude $E = 200\text{ V/m}$. If the potential at the origin $(0,0)$ is zero, find the equation of the equipotential surface passing through point $(1\text{ m}, 0)$.

Solution:

1. Vector electric field: $\vec{E} = E\cos 30^\circ\,\hat{i} + E\sin 30^\circ\,\hat{j} = 200\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\hat{i} + 200\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\hat{j} = 100\sqrt{3}\,\hat{i} + 100\,\hat{j}\text{ V/m}$.

2. Potential function $V(x,y) = -\vec{E}\cdot\vec{r} = -(100\sqrt{3}x + 100y)$.

3. Potential at point $(1,0)$:

$$V(1,0) = -(100\sqrt{3}(1) + 100(0)) = -100\sqrt{3}\text{ Volts}$$

4. The equipotential surface corresponds to $V(x,y) = -100\sqrt{3}$:

$$-(100\sqrt{3}x + 100y) = -100\sqrt{3} \implies \sqrt{3}x + y = \sqrt{3}$$

Exam Check: This is a straight line with slope $m = -\sqrt{3}$ ($\theta = 120^\circ$). Notice field angle was $30^\circ$. $120^\circ - 30^\circ = 90^\circ$, proving $\vec{E}$ is strictly perpendicular to the equipotential line[cite: 13]!
