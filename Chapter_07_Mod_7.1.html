Mean & RMS Values of Alternating Current and EMF

Sinusoidal wave foundations, half-cycle mean ($0.637 I_0$), full-cycle zero identity, Joule heating equivalence ($I_{\text{rms}} = I_0/\sqrt{2}$), and hot-wire ammeter mechanics.

⚡ 01. The Spark (Analogy)

Socho ek carpenter ki aari (saw) ko: jab woh lakdi kaatne ke liye aari ko aage-peeche chalata hai, toh aari ka net displacement ek complete stroke ke baad zero hota hai (Full-cycle average $= 0$). Lekin kya iska matlab lakdi nahi kati? Bilkul kati! Friction ke chalte garmi dono directions ke movement se barabar generate hoti hai. Joule Heating ($H = I^2 R t$) current ki direction par depend nahi karti kyunki $(+I)^2 = (-I)^2 = +I^2$. Isliye AC current ki real power ya effectiveness uske simple arithmetic average se nahi, balki uske Root Mean Square (RMS) value se naapi jaati hai—yani woh equivalent constant DC current jo kisi resistor mein utne hi samay mein exactly utni hi heat generate kare!

📐 02. The Core Law & Formulae

1. Mean (Average) Value of AC

Evaluated over half and full time period $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}$:

$$I_{\text{avg (full)}} = 0$$ $$I_{\text{avg (half)}} = \frac{2}{\pi} I_0 \approx 0.637 I_0, \quad V_{\text{avg (half)}} = \frac{2}{\pi} V_0 \approx 0.637 V_0$$

Definition: That constant direct current which sends the same charge through a circuit in half-cycle time ($T/2$) as is sent by AC.

2. RMS (Virtual / Effective) Value

Root Mean Square evaluated over complete cycle $T$:

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 I_0, \quad V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 V_0$$ $$V_0 = \sqrt{2} V_{\text{rms}} \approx 1.414 V_{\text{rms}}$$

All AC voltmeters, ammeters, and household domestic power ratings (e.g., $220\text{ V}, 50\text{ Hz}$) measure RMS values exclusively!

📝 04. Stepwise Derivations Blueprint
Derivation A

Mean (Average) Value of Alternating Current over Positive Half-Cycle

1. Let the instantaneous alternating current be $i = I_0\sin\omega t$. The charge $dq$ flowing through the circuit in time $dt$ is:

$$dq = i \, dt = (I_0\sin\omega t) dt$$

2. Total charge $q$ that flows during positive half-cycle from $t = 0$ to $t = T/2$ (where $T = 2\pi/\omega$):

$$q = \int_0^{T/2} I_0\sin\omega t \, dt = I_0 \left[ -\frac{\cos\omega t}{\omega} \right]_0^{T/2} = -\frac{I_0}{\omega} \left[ \cos\left(\omega \frac{T}{2}\right) - \cos(0) \right]$$

3. Since $\omega \frac{T}{2} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{T}\right)\left(\frac{T}{2}\right) = \pi$, and $\cos\pi = -1$:

$$q = -\frac{I_0}{\omega} [ -1 - 1 ] = \frac{2 I_0}{\omega} = \frac{2 I_0}{2\pi/T} = \frac{I_0 T}{\pi}$$

4. If $I_{\text{avg}}$ is the mean value of current over this half cycle, by definition $q = I_{\text{avg}} \times \left(\frac{T}{2}\right)$:

$$I_{\text{avg}} \left(\frac{T}{2}\right) = \frac{I_0 T}{\pi} \implies I_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2}{\pi} I_0$$
$$I_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2}{\pi} I_0 \approx 0.637 I_0, \quad V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{2}{\pi} V_0 \approx 0.637 V_0$$

Full Cycle Identity: Over the negative half cycle, $q = -\frac{I_0 T}{\pi}$. Hence, over a complete cycle ($0$ to $T$), total charge $q_{\text{net}} = 0 \implies I_{\text{avg (full)}} = 0$.

Derivation B

Root Mean Square (RMS) Value of AC over a Complete Cycle

Fig 7.0A: Comparing $i(t)$ with squared current $i^2(t)$; squaring keeps the waveform strictly positive with average $\langle i^2\rangle = I_0^2/2$.

1. By Joule's law of heating, heat produced in resistor $R$ during small time $dt$:

$$dH = i^2 R \, dt = (I_0^2\sin^2\omega t) R \, dt$$

2. Total heat produced over a complete cycle of time period $T$:

$$H = \int_0^T I_0^2 R \sin^2\omega t \, dt = I_0^2 R \int_0^T \left( \frac{1 - \cos 2\omega t}{2} \right) dt$$

3. Splitting the integral:

$$H = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2} \left[ \int_0^T dt - \int_0^T \cos 2\omega t \, dt \right]$$

4. Since the integral of $\cos 2\omega t$ over a full period is zero ($\int_0^T \cos 2\omega t \, dt = 0$):

$$H = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2} [T - 0] = \frac{I_0^2 R T}{2}$$

5. If $I_{\text{rms}}$ (effective virtual current) is that steady direct current which produces the exact same heat $H$ in identical resistor $R$ over the same time $T$ ($H = I_{\text{rms}}^2 R T$):

$$I_{\text{rms}}^2 R T = \frac{I_0^2 R T}{2} \implies I_{\text{rms}}^2 = \frac{I_0^2}{2}$$
$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 I_0, \quad V_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707 V_0$$
⚠️ 05. Red Alert Exam Trap Zone

Trap 1: The Domestic $220\text{ V}$ Shock Danger vs Peak Insulation

Domestic mains in India is specified as $220\text{ V}, 50\text{ Hz}$.
• $220\text{ V}$ is strictly the RMS value ($V_{\text{rms}}$).
• The peak voltage that cables and insulation must withstand is: $$V_0 = \sqrt{2} \times 220\text{ V} \approx \mathbf{311.13\text{ Volts}}$$ The voltage swings continuously between $+311\text{ V}$ and $-311\text{ V}$ (total excursion of $622\text{ V}$). This is why $220\text{ V AC}$ is far more lethal and prone to insulation breakdown than $220\text{ V DC}$.

Trap 2: Why Moving Coil Galvanometers Read ZERO in AC

*Board Question:* A standard DC moving coil ammeter is connected in an AC circuit. What will it read?
*Correct Answer:* ZERO! A DC meter produces torque $\tau \propto I$. For AC at $50\text{ Hz}$, current reverses direction 100 times per second. Due to mechanical inertia, the heavy pointer cannot oscillate that fast and remains stationary at the zero mark ($\langle I\rangle_{\text{full}} = 0$).

Trap 3: Hot-Wire Ammeters & Non-Linear Calibration

To measure AC current, Hot-Wire Instruments are used. They operate on heating effect where thermal expansion $\Delta l \propto H \propto I^2$.
Consequence: Since deflection $\theta \propto I^2$, the scale divisions of an AC hot-wire meter are NOT uniform! Graduations are cramped near zero and spread out widely at higher readings.

Trap 4: Frequency of Power Fluctuations in AC

If an alternating current has frequency $f = 50\text{ Hz}$:
• Current $i(t)$ reaches peak twice per cycle $\implies$ current frequency is $50\text{ Hz}$.
• Instantaneous power $p = i^2 R = I_0^2 R \sin^2\omega t = \frac{I_0^2 R}{2}(1 - \cos 2\omega t)$.
• Therefore, power and heat fluctuate with double frequency: $2f = 100\text{ Hz}$! A domestic incandescent bulb flickers 100 times per second.

💡 06. Handcrafted Exemplars
Board Classic Level

Problem: The electric mains in a house is rated at $220\text{ V}, 50\text{ Hz}$. Write down the mathematical equation for the instantaneous alternating voltage. If a heater of resistance $110\text{ }\Omega$ is connected to this supply, find (i) peak current, (ii) RMS current, and (iii) average power dissipated.

Solution:

1. Given: $V_{\text{rms}} = 220\text{ V}$, $f = 50\text{ Hz} \implies \omega = 2\pi f = 100\pi \approx 314.16\text{ rad/s}$.

Peak voltage:

$$V_0 = \sqrt{2} \times V_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 220 \approx 311.08\text{ Volts}$$

Instantaneous voltage equation:

$$v(t) = 311.1 \sin(100\pi t) = 311.1 \sin(314.2 t)\text{ Volts}$$

2. Resistance $R = 110\text{ }\Omega$:

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{R} = \frac{220\text{ V}}{110\text{ }\Omega} = 2.0\text{ Amperes}$$ $$I_0 = \sqrt{2} \times I_{\text{rms}} = 1.414 \times 2.0 = 2.828\text{ Amperes}$$

3. Average power dissipated:

$$P_{\text{avg}} = I_{\text{rms}}^2 R = (2.0)^2 \times 110 = 4 \times 110 = 440\text{ Watts}$$
JEE/NEET Competitive Trap

Problem: An alternating current is given by the superposition equation $i = 3 + 4\sin(\omega t)\text{ A}$, which consists of a steady DC component ($3\text{ A}$) and a pure AC component ($4\sin\omega t\text{ A}$). Calculate the effective (RMS) value of the combined current and the heat produced in a $10\text{ }\Omega$ resistor in $1\text{ minute}$.

Solution:

1. By definition of RMS value, $I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle i^2 \rangle}$ over a complete cycle:

$$i^2 = [3 + 4\sin\omega t]^2 = 9 + 24\sin\omega t + 16\sin^2\omega t$$

2. Evaluate the time average $\langle \dots \rangle$ of each term over a full cycle:

• $\langle 9 \rangle = 9$

• $\langle 24\sin\omega t \rangle = 0$ (linear sine averages to zero)

• $\langle 16\sin^2\omega t \rangle = 16 \times \frac{1}{2} = 8$

$$\langle i^2 \rangle = 9 + 0 + 8 = 17\text{ A}^2$$

3. RMS Current:

$$I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle i^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{17}\text{ A} \approx 4.123\text{ Amperes}$$

General Formula for Composite Current: $I_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{I_{\text{DC}}^2 + \frac{I_0^2}{2}} = \sqrt{3^2 + \frac{4^2}{2}} = \sqrt{9 + 8} = \sqrt{17}\text{ A}$.

4. Total heat produced in $R = 10\text{ }\Omega$ during $t = 60\text{ s}$:

$$H = I_{\text{rms}}^2 R t = (17) \times 10 \times 60 = 10{,}200\text{ Joules} = 10.2\text{ kJ}$$
