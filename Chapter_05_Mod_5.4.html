📐 02. The Core Law & Formulae

Fundamental Magnetic Field Vectors

Magnetization $\vec{M}$, Magnetic Intensity $\vec{H}$, and Total Field $\vec{B}$:

$$\vec{M} = \frac{\vec{m}_{\text{net}}}{V}, \quad \vec{B} = \mu_0(\vec{H} + \vec{M})$$ $$\vec{M} = \chi \vec{H} \implies \vec{B} = \mu_0(1 + \chi)\vec{H} = \mu_r \mu_0 \vec{H}$$

Units: $\vec{M}$ and $\vec{H}$ have SI unit $\text{A/m}$. Relative permeability $\mu_r = 1 + \chi$ (dimensionless).

Curie's Law & Curie-Weiss Law

Thermal disruption of magnetic alignment:

$$\chi_{\text{para}} = \frac{C}{T} \quad (\text{Curie's Law})$$ $$\chi_{\text{ferro}} = \frac{C'}{T - T_c} \quad (T > T_c, \text{Curie-Weiss Law})$$

Where $T_c$ is the Curie Temperature. Above $T_c$, a ferromagnetic substance transitions into a simple paramagnetic substance!

Thermodynamic Law

Deduction of Curie's Law & Ferromagnetic-to-Paramagnetic Transition

1. In a paramagnetic material, individual atomic magnetic dipoles $\vec{m}$ experience an aligning torque from external field $\vec{B}_0$, but thermal kinetic energy ($\sim k_B T$) tends to randomize their orientations.

2. At moderate fields and temperatures where magnetic potential energy is much smaller than thermal energy ($m B_0 \ll k_B T$), statistical Boltzmann mechanics gives net magnetization proportional to applied field and inversely to absolute temperature:

$$M = C \frac{B_0}{T} \implies \frac{M}{H} = \frac{\mu_0 C}{T} \implies \chi = \frac{C}{T}$$

3. Where $C$ is Curie's Constant ($C = \frac{\mu_0 n m^2}{3 k_B}$). As $T \to 0\text{ K}$, magnetization approaches maximum saturation value $M_{\text{sat}} = n m$.

4. In a ferromagnetic substance, atomic exchange coupling creates spontaneous domain alignment below the Curie Temperature $T_c$ (e.g., for Iron $T_c = 1043\text{ K}$ or $770^\circ\text{C}$). Above $T_c$, thermal agitation completely overpowers the exchange coupling, breaking the domain structure into randomized atomic dipoles. The susceptibility then obeys the Curie-Weiss Law:

$$\chi = \frac{C'}{T - T_c} \quad (T > T_c)$$
⚠️ 05. Red Alert Exam Trap Zone

Trap 1: Perfect Diamagnetism & The Superconductor Identity

*Board Question:* What is the magnetic susceptibility of a perfect superconductor?
A superconductor below its critical temperature exhibits the Meissner Effect: it completely expels all magnetic flux from its interior ($\vec{B} = 0$). $$\vec{B} = \mu_0(\vec{H} + \vec{M}) = 0 \implies \vec{M} = -\vec{H}$$ $$\chi = \frac{M}{H} = \mathbf{-1}, \quad \mu_r = 1 + \chi = \mathbf{0}$$ A superconductor is a perfect diamagnet ($\chi = -1, \mu_r = 0$).

Trap 2: Does Diamagnetism depend on Temperature?

*Exam Trap:* "If a piece of bismuth is heated from $300\text{ K}$ to $600\text{ K}$, how does its susceptibility change?"
*Correct Answer:* IT REMAINS COMPLETELY UNCHANGED! Diamagnetism arises from Lenz's law modification of paired electron orbital speeds, which does not depend on thermal agitation. (Only paramagnetism and ferromagnetism depend on temperature).

Trap 3: Liquid in a U-Tube Between Magnetic Poles

• Diamagnetic Liquid (Water/Bismuth): The liquid meniscus in the limb between strong magnetic poles DEPRESSES (goes down) because it is repelled from the strong field.
• Paramagnetic Liquid (Liquid Oxygen/FeSO₄ sol): The meniscus RISES UP because it is attracted towards the region of stronger field.

Trap 4: Electromagnet Core vs Permanent Magnet Materials

• Electromagnet / Transformer Core (Soft Iron): Requires High Permeability $\mu_r$, High Retentivity, but Low Coercivity and thin hysteresis loop to minimize heat dissipation during AC cycling.
• Permanent Magnet (Steel / Alnico): Requires High Retentivity and HIGH COERCIVITY so that stray fields or mechanical drops do not demagnetize it easily.

💡 06. Handcrafted Exemplars
Board Classic Level

Problem: A solenoid has a core of material with relative permeability $\mu_r = 400$. The windings of the solenoid are insulated from the core and carry a current of $2.0\text{ A}$. If the number of turns is $1000\text{ per metre}$, calculate (i) Magnetic Intensity $H$, (ii) Magnetic Field $B$, and (iii) Magnetization $M$ inside the core.

Solution:

1. Magnetic Intensity $H$ depends strictly on the winding free current:

$$H = n I = (1000\text{ m}^{-1}) \times (2.0\text{ A}) = 2.0 \times 10^3\text{ A/m}$$

2. Magnetic field $B$ inside the material core:

$$B = \mu_r \mu_0 H = 400 \times (4\pi \times 10^{-7}\text{ T}\cdot\text{m/A}) \times (2.0 \times 10^3\text{ A/m})$$
$$B = 3200\pi \times 10^{-4}\text{ T} \approx 1.005\text{ Tesla}$$

3. Magnetization $M$:

$$M = \chi H = (\mu_r - 1) H = (400 - 1) \times (2.0 \times 10^3) = 399 \times 2.0 \times 10^3$$
$$M = 7.98 \times 10^5\text{ A/m}$$
JEE/NEET Competitive Trap

Problem: The magnetic susceptibility of a paramagnetic salt is $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ at $300\text{ K}$. At what temperature will its susceptibility increase to $1.8 \times 10^{-5}$? If the sample is placed in a field of $0.6\text{ T}$ and cooled to $4\text{ K}$, it reaches 15% of its saturation magnetization. If the field is increased to $2.0\text{ T}$, can Curie's law still be applied?

Solution:

1. By Curie's Law, $\chi \propto 1/T \implies \chi_1 T_1 = \chi_2 T_2$:

$$T_2 = T_1 \times \left(\frac{\chi_1}{\chi_2}\right) = 300\text{ K} \times \left(\frac{1.2 \times 10^{-5}}{1.8 \times 10^{-5}}\right) = 300 \times \frac{2}{3} = 200\text{ K}$$

2. At $T = 4\text{ K}$ and higher field ($2.0\text{ T}$), the ratio $B/T$ becomes very large ($B/T = 2.0 / 4 = 0.5\text{ T/K}$). Atomic dipoles become almost fully aligned, approaching complete magnetic saturation ($M \to M_{\text{sat}}$). In this regime, magnetization stops increasing linearly with field; therefore, Curie's Law ceases to be valid!
